Selected List of Courses Taught (Sole Instructor), and Descriptions

At University of Illinois Chicago

ENGL 291: Introduction to the Writing of Fiction (Fall 2025, Spring 2026)

This course is an introduction to the art and craft of writing fiction. Fiction is both an ancient and ever-evolving practice that has shaped human expression across cultures, languages, and histories. Through close reading of short stories and novel excerpts from diverse literary traditions, you will encounter a range of narrative strategies—some of which may challenge your assumptions about what a story can be.

During the first half of the semester, we will discuss key elements of craft, such as the sound of writing, character understanding, and the use of point of view. Students will analyze how these elements operate across contemporary literature and will experiment with them in their own writing through imitation, constraint, and play. As Matthew Salesses asks, “How can a writer know what they can do without knowing many different ways that things have been done before?” Writing, like learning a new language, requires risk, failure, and discomfort. Only by stepping into the unfamiliar can we arrive somewhere original. In the process, we will make many mistakes; that is expected and encouraged here. This analytical and imaginative work will transition into an in-person workshop in the second half of the semester. You will submit two original short stories to your peers, who will provide you with substantive feedback and constructive criticism to help you further refine your writing. You will be expected to provide thoughtful commentary on your peers’ work, just as they do for your work, and learn what it means to be a literary citizen and form a literary community.

ENGL 161: Writing about Love (Spring 2025)

In a 2015 New York Times interview, bell hooks said, “I believe wholeheartedly that the only way out of domination is love,” a claim that unsettled some admirers who saw it as a departure from her long-standing critique of “imperialist white supremacy capitalist patriarchy.” In this section of English 161, we will examine the multiple, often competing meanings of what is commonly grouped under the term “love,” from its commodification and manipulation in mass culture to its dismissal as childish, sentimental, futile, and weak. Drawing on readings including bell hooks’s All About Love; “Unruly Edges: Mushrooms as Companion Species” by Anna Tsing; essays by Ross Gay, Aimee Nezhukumatathil, Hanif Abdurraqib, Robin Wall Kimmerer. Students will investigate how love is represented, contested, and mobilized across scholarly and popular contexts. The course is structured around 4 major writing projects that guide students through the research process and culminate in a 10-page research paper focused on an underexamined, misunderstood, or reimagined dimension of love within a chosen social or cultural sphere.

ENGL 160: Finding Your Voice (Summer 2025, Fall 2025)

Every day, the choices we make shape who we are: whom we sit next to in the room, what we pick out at a thrift store, or whether to complain about a loud neighbor on the bus. Our writing, too, reflects these patterns. In this course, you will experiment with different genres—interview transcripts, personal essays, short stories, and argumentative essays—and pay close attention to the patterns that emerge. What surprises you? What feels natural? How does your body react to each genre—does one make you tense up, another let you breathe? Have you unknowingly borrowed speech habits and rhythms from your family, friends, or beloveds? What does authenticity even mean when we write? Through reading, discussion, and four core writing projects, you will explore these questions, cultivating a relationship with language that serves you throughout your college career and beyond. Most of all, you will begin to wonder: What is my voice?

At Indiana University

ENG-W203: Fiction Workshop (Fall 2023)

This course will remind us that fiction has evolved from storytelling, a universal phenomenon, from troglodytes in the Stone Age to merchants in the Renaissance. Each week, students will learn about different craft traditions practiced across countries, cultures, and peoples, and experiment with them. Studying and playing with these various craft practices will eventually lead to the question: through these containers, what story do we want/need to tell, and ultimately, who is the self we strive to express? Like any art, writing asks for an expression of self, not one’s culture. The writer must go deep within, shed her thinking habits and language routines formed in society, and question what she has been taught to do and see. She must read, learn, and, perhaps more importantly, play with different forms to arrive at an unfamiliar, strange place where she feels truthful to herself. Often, writing is like learning a foreign language; only when we feel safe looking ridiculous, not good or correct, but human and messy, can we arrive somewhere new and exciting. In this process, we will commit many mistakes. That is expected and encouraged here.

ENG-W280: Literary Editing and Publishing (Spring 2024)

How much work goes into making a book—and who does it? Using Indiana Review, Graywolf Press, Coffee House Press, and IU Press as case studies, this course explores the contemporary publishing landscape through both research and hands-on practice. Students will learn about manuscript acquisition, developmental editing, copyediting, book design, and marketing while engaging with invited guests, including literary agents, published authors, and publishing professionals.

Throughout the semester, students will write book reviews, evaluate author submissions, and gain experience with editorial decision-making. Working in teams, they will complete a final creative project that simulates the work of a publishing house preparing for a book launch. This project will involve editing a manuscript-in-progress, providing editorial feedback, developing a marketing strategy, and creating supporting materials for publication.
